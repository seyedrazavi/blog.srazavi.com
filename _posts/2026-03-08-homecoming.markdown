title: Homecoming
date: 2026-03-08 12:00:00 Z
categories: [stories]
---
I came home because there was nowhere else to go. University had ended badly in the way that leaves no clean edge to hold onto: a relationship of years dissolving into careful language, the shared future quietly repossessed. I told myself it was temporary. Everyone does. Home as a pause, not a return.

The town accepted me without comment. It had always been good at that. The same low strip mall, the same grocery store with new branding but old aisles, the same roads bending slightly wrong where someone had once decided a tree was more important than symmetry. I slept in my old room, the bed too short now, the walls still holding the faint outline of posters I had torn down in a hurry years earlier. At night the house made its familiar sounds, pipes knocking, floorboards sighing, and it was unsettling how much comfort there was in that. As if I were being told, gently, that nothing had been waiting for me to improve.

During the day I walked. Walking was the only way I could think without turning everything into an argument. I traced the routes I had known by heart at seventeen, past houses where I remembered who had lived there even after they’d gone. I walked the creek path where we used to drink cheap beer and talk about escape with the confidence of people who had never tested gravity. Everything felt smaller, not in a nostalgic way but in a factual one. The town hadn’t changed. I had acquired other measures.

I kept replaying the last months with her, not the fights but the quiet recalibrations. The way conversations about “later” had become conversations about “maybe.” The way she had started asking questions I answered with abstractions. I loved her. That remained true. It just wasn’t sufficient.

My father noticed the walking before he noticed anything else. One evening he called me out to the back steps, where he liked to sit when the light was right. He didn’t ask how I was doing. He never did. He asked if I wanted a beer. I said yes even though I didn’t, because the question wasn’t about thirst.

We sat in silence longer than most people would tolerate. He watched the yard like it might confess something. When he spoke, it wasn’t sudden, but it was deliberate.

“Your mother would tell you to follow your heart,” he said. “And she wouldn’t be wrong. But she wouldn’t tell you the other part.”

I didn’t answer. I had learned early that interrupting him only delayed the point.

“The thing women want most,” he said, carefully, “is to feel safe. Secure. Not romance-safe. Life-safe.” He paused, as if checking that I was still there. “They won’t usually say it like that. They’ll say other things. But it’s underneath all of it.”

I shifted on the concrete. “She said she didn’t care about money,” I said.

He nodded. “She probably meant it. This isn’t about money. It’s about whether your life looks like it can carry another person without collapsing.”

He took a sip of his beer and set it down again untouched. “When you’re young, they don’t need proof. They need direction. They need to see you moving toward something solid. That you can carry your own load. When you can’t, even temporarily, you’re asking them to carry uncertainty for you. That gets heavy fast.”

I remember being struck by how little anger there was in his voice. No judgment, no warning. Just an observation, like weather.

“Sometimes,” he continued, “people chase instability because it feels familiar. Or intense. Sometimes they confuse chaos for depth. Sometimes love pulls them out of that. Sometimes it doesn’t.” He shrugged. “You can’t build your life around exceptions.”

I stared at the yard, at a patch of grass I had scalped with the mower years ago and never fully recovered. “So you’re saying I wasn’t enough.”

He turned then and looked at me properly. “I’m saying you weren’t ready. Those are not the same thing.”

We sat with that. The cicadas started up, the sound thickening the air. After a while he said, almost casually, “The only way a man keeps someone when he can’t stand on his own is by tying them to him somehow. Guilt. Fear. Obligation.” He shook his head. “That’s not love. And you’re not that kind of man.”

That was the closest he ever came to reassurance.

Later, alone in my room, I turned his words over slowly. I didn’t accept them wholesale. They were too clean for that. People are messier than instincts, and love is not an equation you can balance by effort alone. But something in what he said aligned with what I had already begun to suspect. I thought of how often I had asked her to wait without showing her what she was waiting for. How often I had confused potential with progress.

The town did not suddenly make sense after that. It didn’t need to. I took a job that required me to wake up early and show up regardless of mood. I paid my own bills. I stopped narrating my intentions as if narration were action. When I walked now, it was still through the same streets, but I felt less like I was haunting them.

My father didn’t comment on any of this. He didn’t need to. A few months later he got sick. Not dramatically at first. Just enough to change the sound of the house again. We talked less, but the things he had said stayed where I could reach them.

After he died, I returned once more to the back steps alone. The yard looked exactly as it had that evening, which felt unfair and correct at the same time. I understood then that the town hadn’t rejected me, and I hadn’t outgrown it in the way I’d imagined. It had simply held me until I could carry myself.

When I think about her now, I don’t replay the ending. I replay the middle, the years where love was real but insufficient. I understand better what was missing, not virtue, not feeling, but weight-bearing structure. The kind you don’t notice until you need it.

That summer did not fix me. It did something quieter. It taught me that love does not fail because people stop wanting each other, but because wanting is asked to substitute for standing. And some places, towns, relationships, people, can only hold you for so long before you have to learn how to hold yourself.
